1. GOOGLE / ALPHABET
Subject: Search grew 17%. Capex doubled. Which number is the business?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Hello,

I am writing about an entry-level or associate role in strategy, business
operations, or finance at Google. I studied business management and I work in
German and English.

Rather than describe myself, here is the question I have been sitting with.

In Q2, Alphabet did $119.8 billion in revenue, up 24 percent. Search and other
was $63.27 billion, up 17. Cloud was up 82 with a $514 billion backlog. AI Mode
passed a billion monthly users. By any read of the demand side, the AI
transition is going well.

And the stock fell — on the capex line. Full-year guidance moved to $195–205
billion, up from $180–190, with second-quarter capex doubling year over year.

I find that gap more interesting than either number alone. It suggests the
market has stopped asking whether Google keeps the queries. A billion monthly
users settled that. It has started asking what a query now costs. A blue-link
result had close to zero marginal cost. An AI answer does not. So the number
that decides the next five years is not query volume or even Search revenue —
it is the spread between revenue per AI query and cost per AI query, and
whether that spread widens or narrows as adoption grows.

Growth that costs more per unit than it returns is a different business than
the one that made Google, even when both are called growth. I would want to
work where that spread is measured.

I am at the start of my career and have no track record yet. What I can offer
is that I read primary filings rather than coverage, I work in two languages,
and I will do the unglamorous analysis nobody volunteers for. I am not able to
work in the US at present and would need sponsorship and relocation.

If nothing fits, I would value fifteen minutes or one piece of advice on what to
build over the next two years. I will take a hard answer as readily as a kind one.

2. APPLE
Subject: Apple raised volume and price in the same quarter. Almost nobody can.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Hello,

I am writing about an entry-level or associate role in finance, business
operations, or strategy at Apple. I studied business management and I work in
German and English.

Here is what I have been working on.

I have been studying pricing power across consumer categories. The clearest
failure case I found is Nike: it now sells more running shoes than On while
carrying an $83 average selling price against On's $143, and its FY26 gross
margin only reads healthy because roughly 900 basis points of it came from a
one-off $986 million tariff recovery. Volume recovered. Price did not.

Apple is the inverse, and the detail I find most interesting is not the
headline. In Q2 iPhone shipments rose 13 percent while average selling price
rose 8, and iPhone took a record 49 percent of global smartphone revenue.
Volume and price moved up together, which most companies are told is a
trade-off.

What makes it unusual is how. Through the memory shortage, competitors raised
list prices to cover bill-of-materials costs and Apple largely did not. So the
ASP increase did not come from moving the ladder — it came from moving
customers up it. That is a mix outcome, not a pricing action, and it is far more
durable, because a list-price increase can be matched by a competitor in a
quarter and a mix shift cannot.

I would want to work on the question that follows: how far up that ladder is
there still room, and what happens to Services attach rates as the mix moves.

I am at the start of my career and have no track record yet. What I offer is
that I read primary filings rather than coverage, and that I will do the
analysis nobody volunteers for. I am not able to work in the US at present and

3. AMAZON
Subject: The $19.8bn business nobody writes about
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Hello,

I am writing about an entry-level or associate role in finance or business
operations at Amazon. I studied business management and I work in German and
English.

Here is the argument I have been working on.

Q2 was Amazon's first $200 billion quarter — $200.6 billion, up 20 percent —
and almost all of the commentary went to AWS, fairly: $42.2 billion, up 37,
accelerating for a fifth straight quarter, with operating margin up 650 basis
points to 39.

The line I keep returning to is advertising. $19.8 billion, up 26 percent. That
is under half of AWS in revenue, but it does not sit in the cloud segment — it
sits on top of the retail business, and marketplace advertising carries margin
that retail itself never will. In practical terms it is the retail segment's
profit engine wearing a different name.

Which makes the interesting question a limit question rather than a growth one.
Advertising on a marketplace is monetised attention, and attention is the same
asset that makes the marketplace worth advertising on. Every incremental
sponsored placement earns revenue now and takes a small amount of trust from
the discovery experience that produced the demand. Nobody knows exactly where
that line sits, and the segment reporting is not built to show it.

I would want to work on measuring it, because a business that can only be grown
by degrading its own input has a ceiling that does not appear in a growth rate.

I am at the start of my career and have no track record yet. What I offer is
that I read primary filings rather than coverage, and that I will take on the
unglamorous work. I am not able to work in the US at present and would need

4. STARBUCKS
Subject: Back to Starbucks is a pricing strategy, and China is the proof
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Hello,

I am writing about an entry-level or associate role in strategy or business
operations at Starbucks. I studied business management and I work in German and
English.

Here is why Starbucks specifically.

I have been studying pricing power in consumer brands. The failure case is
Nike: more pairs sold than On, at an $83 average selling price against On's
$143, with a gross margin that only looks healthy because roughly 900 basis
points came from a one-off tariff recovery. Volume returned; price did not.

Back to Starbucks reads to me as the deliberate opposite. A simplified menu,
fresh-baked food, handwritten cups, faster service — those are not four service
improvements, they are one decision to be worth more rather than to sell more
cheaply. Every one of them is a cost line that only pays if the customer accepts
a higher price for the experience. Q3 comparable sales of 7.9 percent against
5.7 expected suggests they do.

The part I find most instructive is China. Forming the joint venture and
licensing roughly 90 percent of international locations is a decision to treat
China as a market to be designed for locally rather than a distribution problem
to be managed from headquarters. That is exactly the choice Nike has not made,
and it is why Greater China fell 17 percent last quarter while Starbucks China
grew 7. The same brand problem, two different theories of ownership, and one is
visibly working.

I would want to work on which categories can carry the higher price next, so
the strategy keeps producing proof rather than only patience.

HOW TO SEND THESE
================================================================================

LinkedIn, to a named person. Search the company plus "University Recruiter",
"Early Careers", "Talent Acquisition", or the function you are writing about
(Corporate Strategy, Business Operations, FP&A). Send the letter as a message.
A connection request has a 300-character note limit — send the request first,
then the full letter once accepted.

Or paste it into the cover-letter field of the portal:
  Google      google.com/about/careers
  Apple       jobs.apple.com
  Amazon      amazon.jobs
  Starbucks   starbucks.com/careers

Not to press@, info@, or support@. Those go nowhere useful.


WHY THESE FOUR ARE BUILT THE WAY THEY ARE

Each one names a number the company reports, then says something about that
number the company's own press release does not say. That is the entire
mechanism. A recruiter reads a hundred letters that say "I am passionate about
your mission" and none that say "your capex doubled and here is the question
that implies".

If you write more of these, keep the rule: find one real, checkable, recent
number, and say the non-obvious thing about it. If you cannot find the
non-obvious thing, pick a different company. The letter is only as good as that
one paragraph.
